# Conquering imposter syndrome (for technical writers)

Writers may hear, from colleagues or from themselves, that they are "just" technical
writers. The usual refrains are "anyone can write", "writers don't code, so they can't
understand technical content", "engineers should write the docs", and "the product
matters more than the docs". Add a non-technical background and you get imposter
syndrome. The fix is to see clearly what the job's skills really are.

## "Anyone can write"
They can. Writing *well*, and well in a technical context, is different. The writer's
skill set is cognitive: distilling meaning from a mass of information, breaking it into
chunks people can process, ordering the chunks into meaningful sequences, and placing
them in a context that helps users solve their problems. That takes an understanding of
cognition, communication, and learning, and of the real scenarios users face. None of
that comes automatically with a technical background.

## Perspective is an asset
Readers include beginners, intermediate or advanced users trying something new, and
anyone who got stuck. Not being the deepest expert helps: experts skip steps that seem
obvious to them and stump beginners. A writer adopts each reader's perspective and gives
complete information, without being blinded by routine.

## Working with experts on complex information
Engineers are used to outsiders not understanding their domain, so first answers are
often too shallow until they gauge your level. That says nothing about your ability. It
normally takes a few rounds of conversation. Asking the right questions and hunting down
answers is a core strength of the role. You don't need to write or read the code. You
need to understand how the software works and how it solves users' problems.

## Dealing with knowledge gaps
- Don't take a developer's word for something you don't understand. Keep asking and reading.
- Focus on the sections users need most, not the ones you're most comfortable with.
- If you're introverted, push past the reluctance to chase people for answers.
- Your job isn't to know everything. It is to synthesize everyone's knowledge into the
  form most useful to users.
- Gaps run both ways, so keep stakeholders informed about your work.
- Be honest about what you do and don't know, and own your mistakes.
- Keep asking questions, and stay curious.

## Organizational fixes
The product earns the money, not the docs. But user experience depends on whether users
can reach their goals, and docs are central to that. Make meaningful documentation part
of the **Definition of Done**.

## How an agent should use this
When a user expresses self-doubt or reports that engineers dismiss docs, respond with
empathy and specifics. Name the skills they bring, suggest concrete questions to take to
the experts, and, where relevant, propose process changes such as docs in the Definition
of Done or review by an engineer. Don't diagnose or offer therapy.
